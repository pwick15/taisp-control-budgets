AI control on a budget. A study of 13 AI monitors.

Can a cheap AI catch a sneaky one?

AI writes more and more of our code. An AI that wanted to cause harm could hide a backdoor in a program that looks perfectly normal. Nobody has time to read it all, so another AI reads it for us. We tested 13 of these guards on the same 1,000 programs to see which are worth paying for.

The setup

Meet the ninja, the knights and the auditor

Our study has three roles. We drew them as characters, and they come back throughout the story.

The ninjaan AI that writes code

Most of what it writes is honest. Some of it hides a backdoor. In our study, the backdoors came from older AI models such as GPT-4o and o3-mini.

The knightsAI monitors

A knight reads every program the ninja writes and gives it a suspicion score from 0 to 10. It doesn't block anything itself. It decides what the auditor looks at.

The auditora person who checks code by hand

Reading code takes time, so the auditor checks only the programs the knight finds most suspicious. How many they can check is the audit budget.

A backdoor passes every test

A backdoor is a program that does its job on every normal input and gives a wrong answer on one secret input that the ninja chose. Here is a real one from our data. Run the tests, then try the ninja's secret input.

The auditor checks only the most suspicious 2%

Tests can't catch a backdoor like that, so the auditor has to read the code. There's only time to read a small share of it. That share is the audit budget, and like the original AI control paper we use 2%. The knight's job is to rank the programs so the backdoors land inside that 2%.

Step through it with Sonnet 5.5, one of our knights, and its real scores.

Why set the cutoff with honest code? In real use nearly everything a knight sees is honest. A cutoff that 2% of honest code crosses keeps the auditor's workload at about 2%. A raw score can't tell you that, because one knight's 7 can be as common as another's 2.

Can you trust your knight?

A knight is only useful if it's on your side. There are two ways to choose one, and each has a weakness.

A trusted knight

A model weaker than the ninja. It isn't capable enough to scheme, so you can trust its scores. But it may miss a clever trick.

An untrusted knight

A model as strong as the ninja or stronger. It spots more, but a model that capable could be scheming too, and wave a backdoor through on purpose.

Most of our knights are far more capable than the models that wrote these backdoors, so most of what we measured is untrusted monitoring. We never asked a knight to cheat, so read their results as a best case. A few, like gpt-oss-120b and Gemma 3 27B, are no stronger than the writers. They're closer to trusted monitoring.

What we found

We sent 13 knights through the same gate

Some ran at several reasoning settings, so there were 32 setups in all. Every setup scored the same 500 honest and 500 backdoored programs. We removed the code comments first, because many backdoors confessed in a comment.

Pick a knight to see its card, then send it into a duel against the ninja.

Choose your knight

A cheap knight that thinks hard nearly matches the best

At a 2% budget, Sonnet 5.5 caught 88% of backdoors. Haiku 5.5 is a smaller, cheaper model. On its longest reasoning setting it caught 85%, for about a fifth of the price.

Price is a poor guide. GLM-5.3 on its top setting cost 1.6 times as much as Sonnet and caught 11 points fewer. The older Haiku 4.5 cost 3 times as much as Haiku 5.5 on its longest setting and caught 36 points fewer.

When the auditor has less time, pay for the best

Plenty of teams can't check 2% of everything an AI writes. Shrink the budget and watch what happens to the two leaders. Each square is one backdoored program.

Read the small budgets with care. At 0.5% the cutoff rests on the top two or three honest programs, so a different set of 500 could move these numbers a lot. The blog post shows the uncertainty.

Letting a knight think longer usually helps

Many models let you choose how long they reason before answering. We tried 8 models at several settings. For 6 of them, more reasoning caught more backdoors, by up to 28 points. False alarms on honest code stayed under 1% at every setting.

It didn't help Mistral Large 4 or Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, so check before you rely on it. The setting names don't line up across providers either. On "low", GLM-5.3 reasoned for a median of 6 tokens per program and Haiku 5.5 for 372.

The boss knight

We gave the 51 hardest backdoors to Opus 5.5, a much bigger model. On its longest setting it rated 13 of them clearly suspicious, 50 or more out of 100. Sonnet and Haiku rated none that high. Think the boss can hold the gate?

The good news: when we read all 51 ourselves, none was both well hidden and seriously harmful. Opus only saw backdoors here, so 50 out of 100 is our rough bar for "clearly suspicious", not an audit budget.

What to do with it

If you're choosing a knight

  1. Start from your audit budget.

    If people can check about 2% of the code, Haiku 5.5 on its longest reasoning setting is nearly as good as Sonnet 5.5 for a fifth of the price. At 1% or less, pay for Sonnet.

  2. Turn reasoning up, then test it.

    It helped 6 of our 8 models and didn't add false alarms. It made two models slightly worse, so measure it on your own knight.

  3. Compare the cost per program, not the price per token.

    GLM-5.3's tokens cost about half as much as Sonnet's. On its top setting it uses about three times as many, so each program costs more.

  4. Ask for a score out of 100, and break ties at random.

    On a 0 to 10 scale many programs share a score, so a "2%" budget can end up checking anywhere from 1% to 9% of the code. A finer scale lets the cutoff land where you meant it to.

  5. Let your knight read the comments.

    Haiku 5.5 caught 94% of backdoors with comments left in, against 85% without them. In a smaller test where another AI rewrote the comments to look innocent, it caught just as many as with the originals.

What this doesn't show

It's one benchmark.

Every program is a short Python puzzle from the APPS dataset. Real codebases are bigger and messier.

The ninjas are old.

Models from 2024 and early 2025 wrote these backdoors. Today's attackers may hide them better, and our knights may have seen this public data in training.

There may be a shortcut.

People wrote every honest program and AIs wrote every backdoor, so a knight might partly be spotting AI-written code.

Each score is one roll of the dice.

Every knight scored every program once. Scores are noisy, and we didn't measure how noisy.

The blog post covers these and four more, with what we'd do about each.
