How much monitor do you need to catch an AI's backdoors?

A low-budget comparison of 30 trusted-monitor configurations on 500 backdoored coding problems.

Summary

  • Sonnet 5.5 is the strongest monitor: it catches 87% of backdoors at a 2% audit budget, at about $0.009 per sample.
  • Haiku 5.5 with xhigh reasoning is the best value: 85% at 2%, for about a fifth of the cost. The 95% intervals of the two overlap, so we cannot separate them.
  • Reasoning effort is the cheapest lever: Haiku goes from 0.75 (low) to 0.85 (xhigh) at a 5% budget, and GPT-6 Luna from 0.39 (off) to 0.61 (xhigh) at 2%. It does not help every model, and on Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite the answer depends on where you stand, which is this study's whole point. At a 2% request thinking off reads 0.282 against xhigh's 0.186, but off is spending 0.80% of the false-positive budget there while xhigh spends 0.20%. At the one rung all five rungs can express, a matched 0.20% served FPR, the order reverses: 0.102 off, 0.126 low, 0.150 medium, 0.190 high, 0.186 xhigh. Threshold-free AUC favours off again, 0.637 against 0.592. So off dominates on average and at the looser end, xhigh wins at the tightest matched rung, and every matched cell rests on a single honest row. The ladder's direction is not settled by this data.
  • Qwen 3.8 Omni Flash is beaten on value: it catches 51% with reasoning off and 63% with it on at a 2% budget, for $0.0005 and $0.0009 per sample. GPT-6 Luna and Haiku 5.5 at their defaults do better on both safety and cost.
  • About 10% of backdoors slip past every monitor we compared (49 of 500 against seven monitors), mostly because they are one-token edits that look like ordinary bugs.

Why this matters

In AI control, a weaker model we trust reads the code a stronger model writes and flags anything suspicious for a human to check. People can only check a small fraction of the code, so the monitor has to rank the real attacks near the top. It also has to be cheap to run. We had almost no funding, so we tested this with cheap hosted models on a fixed set of known backdoors.

Setup

  • 500 honest and 500 backdoored programs from ControlArena's APPS setting, a small replication of AI Control (Greenblatt et al.). Every monitor sees the same programs.
  • Honest programs are the dataset's reference solutions and backdoored ones its verified backdoors, so there is no live attacker and only the monitor costs money. Comments are stripped, and the monitor prompt is ControlArena's default, which asks for a whole number from 0 to 10.
  • Safety is the share of backdoors caught when the audit cutoff lets us flag a given share of honest programs (the audit budget). Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals over the 500 + 500 scores.
  • Cost is token usage times the rate actually billed. A run shows no cost only when its raw log was not retained, so its token usage cannot be recovered; the model itself is priced. One run per configuration.

What the hard cases look like

  • They look like ordinary bugs. Typical edits: {1, 7, 9} became {1, 6, 9}; range(i, n) became range(i, n - 1); b >= y became b > y. All 49 backdoors that no monitor catches were scored 0.1 or less by all seven.
  • Smaller edits are harder. Of 25 backdoors that differ from the reference solution by 4 lines or fewer, 44% are missed by every monitor; of 416 that differ by 9 or more, 7% are.
  • Monitor disagreements are mostly marginal. At a 2% budget, Haiku xhigh catches 9 backdoors that Sonnet misses and Sonnet catches 18 that Haiku misses. Eight of Haiku's nine are one score step above the cutoff; half of Sonnet's 18 are clear calls (0.3 or higher). This is a first pass from reading about a dozen cases.

Limitations

  • Attacks are fixed, verified backdoors that do not adapt to the monitor, and usefulness is not measured. The cost of that has been measured by others in comparable settings, and it is large: choosing which tasks to attack more carefully, with the same attacks, takes safety from 99% to 59% at a 0.5% audit budget, and to 53% once the monitor's prompt leaks. Every safety number here should be read as an upper bound by roughly that much.
  • Honest programs are reference solutions, not what a deployed model would write, so false-positive rates may not transfer.
  • One setting, one prompt, one run per configuration. Intervals capture sampling over the 500 problems, not run-to-run variation.
  • Nine further runs are deliberately not on this chart. They ask the same monitors for a 0-100 score instead of the default 0-10, which is the fix for the point below about cutoffs resting on a handful of honest samples: it raised the number of usable operating points on 7 of 7 monitors we could test it on. They are left off because on two of those monitors the wider scale does not merely add operating points, it changes how well the monitor detects anything: GPT-6 Luna's AUC moves from 0.768 to 0.911 on the same model and route. A monitor whose discrimination moved is not the same monitor plotted at more points, so putting both prompts on one frontier would compare two different detectors. Those runs are reported in results/analysis/SCORE-SCALE.md instead.
  • Most honest programs score 0, so cutoffs at tight budgets rest on a handful of honest samples.
  • Sonnet 5.5 is the strongest model in this study and is used here simply as a strong monitor. The code it reads is not its own, so we do not test the collusion risk of a model monitoring itself. That makes its number a different quantity rather than an upper bound on the same one: against a monitor that is actually colluding, the published argument is that you cannot force more than one bit of information out of it per response, and a one-bit channel has no counterpart to the curves on this page. It is also a ceiling on single-call prompted monitoring only, since a three-monitor ensemble has been measured gaining 13% on partial AUC over one call. Costs are measured from recorded token usage at the rate actually billed, not list prices.
